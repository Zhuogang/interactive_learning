# Principles

> **Pull ideas out of your head, a little every day, in your own words.**

This toolkit rests on three ideas:

| | From | What it gives us |
|---|---|---|
| 🧠 **Learning science** | Decades of memory research (Roediger, Karpicke, Bjork, Dunlosky and many others) | *What* to do in a study session: retrieve, space it out, mix it up, guess first, ask why, check yourself. |
| 🌱 **Tiny Habits & Mini Habits** | BJ Fogg (2019) and Stephen Guise (2013) | *How* to keep showing up: an anchor, a minimum too small to fail, a celebration, bonus reps. |
| 🧒 **The Feynman technique** | A popular distillation of how Richard Feynman approached understanding | *How you know* you understand: explain it simply, and let the gaps tell you what to study next. |

Each principle below has three parts: **the idea**, **the evidence**, and **in this toolkit**, which is the concrete mechanic it becomes. The [starter deck](projects/learning-how-to-learn/) quizzes you on this page, so you learn the method by using it.

## 1. Retrieval practice

*Pull, don't push.*

**The idea.** Each time you pull an idea out of memory (answering a question, writing down what you remember, explaining it out loud), the memory gets stronger and easier to find next time. Putting it in again by rereading, highlighting or rewatching feels productive but does much less. Retrieval also tells you, honestly, what you don't know yet.

**The evidence.** In Roediger & Karpicke (2006), students read a short passage either four times, or once followed by three recall tests. Five minutes later, the rereaders did better. A week later, the tested group remembered far more (about 61% vs. 40%), even though the rereaders had predicted they would remember more. Karpicke & Roediger (2008) found that once a word had been recalled, studying it again added almost nothing a week later, while testing it again raised recall from roughly a third to about 80%. In a review of ten popular study techniques, Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated *practice testing* as one of only two "high utility" techniques (the other is spacing). Rereading and highlighting were rated low utility.

**In this toolkit.** Everything is a question. Short-answer cards ask you to produce the answer (type it, or say it out loud) before you reveal anything. The answer is never shown before you try.

## 2. Spacing

*Let a little forgetting happen.*

**The idea.** Spread study over days instead of cramming it into one sitting. Coming back after you've started to forget makes recall harder, and that harder recall is what makes the memory last. Each time you succeed, the next gap can be longer.

**The evidence.** The spacing effect goes back to Ebbinghaus (1885) and is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. A meta-analysis of more than 300 experiments (Cepeda et al., 2006) confirms that spaced practice beats massed practice for long-term retention. The longer you need to remember something, the longer the ideal gaps between sessions (Cepeda et al., 2008). Cramming can work for tomorrow morning; it fades quickly after that.

**In this toolkit.** A simple Leitner system (Leitner, 1972). Every card sits in a box, and the box decides when you see it next:

| Box | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Next review in** | 1 day | 3 days | 1 week | 2 weeks | 1 month | 2 months |

- **Got it**: the card moves up one box.
- **Partly**: it stays in the same box.
- **Missed**: it goes back to box 1. If you keep going, it also comes back later in the same session.
- Only your first answer of the day can move a card up.
- A correct answer you were *guessing* counts as "partly". Luck isn't knowledge.

## 3. Tiny Habits

*Anchor it, shrink it, celebrate it.*

**The idea.** BJ Fogg's behavior model is **B = MAP**: a **B**ehavior happens when **M**otivation, **A**bility and a **P**rompt come together at the same moment. If any one is missing, nothing happens. Motivation is unreliable (it surges and fades), so don't design for it. Design for ability by making the behavior tiny, and design the prompt by attaching it to an **anchor**: something you already do every day. Then celebrate immediately, with a fist pump or a "Yes!". In Fogg's words, *emotions create habits*. The feeling of success right after the behavior (he calls it *Shine*) is what wires it in.

The recipe:

> **After I** _[anchor]_, **I will** _[tiny behavior]_. Then I'll _[celebrate]_.

**The evidence.** Fogg's book distills his Stanford Behavior Design Lab work and his experience coaching people through the Tiny Habits program. It is a practitioner framework, not a single experiment. The recipe is a form of *implementation intention*, an "if–then" plan. A meta-analysis of 94 studies found that implementation intentions have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

**In this toolkit.** Each deck has a habit recipe you fill in: *After I ___, I will answer one card.* When you reach your daily minimum, the app stops and celebrates. That is on purpose.

## 4. Mini Habits

*Too small to fail. Bonus reps welcome.*

**The idea.** Stephen Guise's version: choose a daily requirement that is "stupid small", like one push-up, one page or one card, and never miss it. Anything beyond the minimum is a **bonus rep**: welcome, never required. A day with only the minimum is a full success. Because the bar is so low, no excuse is good enough to skip it. And because starting is the hardest part, one card often turns into twenty. When it starts to feel easy, don't raise the minimum. Put the extra energy into bonus reps.

**The evidence.** Guise's book grew out of his own "one push-up challenge". He explains it in terms of saving willpower. The "ego depletion" research he draws on has had replication trouble, so treat that explanation with caution; the practical advice holds up without it. Habit research supports the no-shame part. In Lally et al. (2010), people took a median of 66 days (range 18–254) for a daily behavior to become automatic, and missing a single day did not materially set them back.

**In this toolkit.** The default daily minimum is **one card**. Progress counts *show-up days*, not a fragile streak. Missed days carry no penalty and need no make-up sessions. If cards pile up, the minimum is still one.

## 5. Pretesting

*Guess before you learn.*

**The idea.** Try to answer before you've been taught, even if you're wrong. A failed attempt that is followed by the right answer makes that answer stick better than just studying. Guessing switches on what you already know and makes you curious, so you notice the answer when it arrives.

**The evidence.** Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009) found that students who took a test on a passage *before* reading it learned the tested material better than students who spent that time studying, even on questions they got wrong. Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009) found that unsuccessful retrieval attempts improved later learning, as long as the correct answer followed. The *generation effect* is closely related: producing an answer yourself beats reading it (Slamecka & Graf, 1978).

**In this toolkit.** Each new concept starts with a pretest card, labeled as a guess. A pretest miss doesn't count against you (the card just starts in box 1), and the answer is shown right after.

## 6. Elaboration

*Ask why and how.*

**The idea.** Don't just store a fact. Ask "Why is this true?" and "How does this work?", and answer in your own words. Explaining connects the new idea to what you already know, and connected ideas are easier to understand and have more routes back to them.

**The evidence.** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated two related techniques as moderate utility. *Elaborative interrogation* means asking and answering "why?". *Self-explanation* means explaining how new information relates to what you know, or explaining each step while you solve a problem. Chi et al. (1994) found that students prompted to explain each line of a biology text to themselves understood it better than students who read it twice.

**In this toolkit.** Every concept has "why / how" cards. Short-answer cards come with key points, so you can check whether your explanation contains the ideas that matter.

## 7. Interleaving

*Mix it up.*

**The idea.** Practice different kinds of problems or ideas mixed together, rather than in blocks of one kind at a time. Mixing feels harder and slower, but it trains the skill that real problems demand: recognizing *which* idea or method applies, and telling similar ones apart.

**The evidence.** In Rohrer & Taylor (2007), students practiced volume problems for four kinds of solids. Those who practiced in blocks did better during practice, but those who practiced the same problems shuffled scored far higher on a test a week later (about 63% vs. 20%). In Kornell & Bjork (2008), people learning painters' styles were better at recognizing the artists of new paintings after interleaved study, yet most of them believed blocked study had worked better.

**In this toolkit.** Review queues rotate between concepts instead of finishing one at a time, and "compare" cards ask you to tell related concepts apart.

## 8. Calibration

*Don't fool yourself.*

**The idea.** We're poor judges of what we know. Rereading and recognizing make material feel familiar, and familiarity feels like knowing. This is an *illusion of competence*. The fix is to commit to an answer and a confidence rating *before* you see the answer. Rating afterwards is distorted by hindsight ("I knew that"). Over time you learn where your sense of knowing is right and where it misleads you. There's a bonus too: errors you were confident about are the ones you're most likely to correct. This is the *hypercorrection effect*.

**The evidence.** Koriat & Bjork (2005) describe illusions of competence during study. Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013) review how learners' beliefs about learning mislead them. Karpicke, Butler & Roediger (2009) found that most students report rereading as a main study strategy and few test themselves. Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001) showed that errors made with high confidence are corrected best once feedback arrives. Feynman (1974) put it most simply: *"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."*

**In this toolkit.** Before every reveal, you tap *Guessing*, *I think so* or *I know it*. The app shows how often you're right at each level, and it counts a correct guess as "partly".

## 9. Desirable difficulties

*Hard feels slow, but works.*

**The idea.** Robert Bjork's name for difficulties that slow down apparent progress but make learning last: retrieving instead of rereading, spacing, interleaving and guessing first. The catch is that they *feel* worse, so learners pick the easy option. It helps to separate **performance**, meaning how well practice is going right now, from **learning**, meaning what lasts and transfers to new situations. A difficulty is only desirable if you can meet it with effort. If you lack the background, it is just frustration, so go back to the prerequisites.

**The evidence.** Bjork (1994) introduced the term. Bjork & Bjork (2011) give an accessible overview, and Soderstrom & Bjork (2015) review the learning-versus-performance distinction.

**In this toolkit.** Sessions mix concepts, missed cards come back, and you're asked to produce answers rather than recognize them. Getting 60% right is normal and healthy. Concepts are ordered by prerequisites so the difficulty stays manageable.

## 10. The Feynman technique

*Teach it simply. Your gaps are your syllabus.*

**The idea.**

1. Pick a concept.
2. Explain it in plain words, without notes, as if to a curious 12-year-old.
3. Notice where you got stuck, reached for jargon, or hand-waved. Those are your gaps.
4. Go back to the source, fill the gaps, and explain again, more simply this time. An analogy helps.

Naming a thing is not explaining it. "Spacing works because of the spacing effect" explains nothing.

**The evidence.** Feynman never wrote this method down. It is a popular distillation of how he worked. David Goodstein recalls Feynman failing to prepare a freshman lecture on a deep topic and concluding: *"I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we really don't understand it"* (Goodstein & Goodstein, 1996). Research backs up the parts. People overestimate how well they understand how everyday things work until they try to explain them step by step; this is the *illusion of explanatory depth* (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). Teaching without notes improves the teacher's own learning, largely because it forces retrieval (Koh, Lee & Lim, 2018).

**In this toolkit.** Key concepts end with a *teach-it* card. It sets an audience, a word limit, the jargon to avoid and a rubric. The app flags jargon as you write and shows the follow-up questions a curious kid would ask. With an AI tutor, "check my explanation" gets you a coach who asks questions instead of rewriting your words. Every gap you find can become a new card.

## The concept ladder

Each concept in a deck climbs the same ladder. These are the `purpose` values in a deck file.

| Step | Card `purpose` | What you do | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Guess | `pretest` | Answer before learning | Pretesting |
| 2. Read | *(concept intro)* | Read the summary and the pointed-to part of the source | — |
| 3. Recall | `recall` | Pull the core idea from memory | Retrieval |
| 4. Why / how | `explain` | Explain the mechanism in your own words | Elaboration |
| 5. Use it | `apply` | Apply it to a new situation | Transfer |
| 6. Tell apart | `compare` | Distinguish it from a related concept | Interleaving |
| 7. Teach it | `teach` | Explain it simply to a novice | Feynman |

After that, the cards come back on the spacing schedule, mixed with other concepts.

## The daily loop

```
  anchor ──► open the app (or say "session") ──► 1 card ──► 🎉 celebrate
                                                              │
                                     stop here: it counts ◄───┴───► bonus reps
```

1. **Anchor.** An existing routine reminds you: *After I pour my coffee…*
2. **Minimum.** One card. Reviews that are due come first, then new cards.
3. **Celebrate.** The app pauses to say so. Mean it.
4. **Choose.** Stop (that's a full success) or keep going (bonus reps).
5. **Gaps become cards.** Something confused you? Say so, and it becomes a new question.

## Anti-patterns

- **Rereading and highlighting as your main strategy.** They feel productive but build little. (§1, §8)
- **Cramming.** It works for tomorrow, then fades. (§2)
- **The 300-card day one.** Build *just in time*: one unit, or a handful of concepts, at a time.
- **Raising the minimum.** That brings back the resistance the tiny minimum removed. (§4)
- **Streak guilt.** Show-up days, not streaks. Miss a day and just come back. (§4)
- **Peeking.** Revealing before committing to an answer turns retrieval into reading. (§1, §8)
- **Memorizing what you don't understand.** Understand first, then remember (Wozniak, 1999). (§6, §10)
- **Collecting decks instead of studying.** One active deck beats ten unopened ones.

## Honest caveats

- The strongest, most replicated evidence is for **retrieval practice** and **spacing**. Interleaving, pretesting and elaboration are well supported, but their effects depend more on the material and the learner.
- Tiny Habits and Mini Habits are **practitioner frameworks**. We use them as design heuristics, not as laws of nature.
- **Decks built by an AI can contain mistakes.** That's why every card carries a `ref` into the source. If a card looks wrong, check the source and fix the card.
